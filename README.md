Aspiring college students interested in the field of data science often struggle to know where to start their career path. The expertise in programming, communication, and statistics needed to succeed in this area of study make it especially difficult[^1]. This work presents the best practices of where to get the technical and networking skills along with making a profile to become a data scientist, starting from college.

# Skill-Building Activities
This section discusses activities that increase your proficiency for skills that a data scientist needs to succeed.

## Doing LeetCode
Solving LeetCode questions can be great practice for applying your coding concepts into retrievable skills[^2]. These questions also help you prepare for the problems given in technical interviews. It sharpens your general problem-solving skills and improves technical confidence. 

Start with the Python questions in LeetCode to get a grasp of how to use the language and its libraries. Then do SQL problems to learn how to handle datasets. Set time limits for how long you can solve a certain problem (depending on its difficulty) to mimic an interview environment. When enough time has passed, look at another person's work for an optimal solution to learn from.

## Practicing Kaggle
In Kaggle, you can work with real-world datasets in competitions with a community to improve your own solution[^3]. Sanjay Penmetsa who is a data scientist at the Omeros company said that, "I ended up in biotech, but you can, let's say you are interested in like a construction company, data science can be applied there. You are interested in e-commerce, data science applies there" [^4]. Kaggle provides datasets from numerous academic fields, making it accessible to get acquainted with other industries to pair with data science.

Use the learn section of Kaggle to get introduced to core concepts of Python and SQL. Build up from there to do competitions designed for beginners like the Titanic[^5].

# Networking and Practical Experience
This section covers different avenues to build your network with people in the field of technology.

## Competing in Hackathons
Taking part in these events helps develop collaboration skills because you must work in a team to build a product under pressure, similar to a real-world environment. This facilitates meeting people in the industry like employers and mentors who you can connect to on platforms like LinkedIn.

Go to UWB Hacks[^6] as a starting hackathon as it is suited for people of all skill levels. Show up to supplementary events aside from the hackathon like the workshops. Form a team before March as this event happens around April. Follow-up with the people you meet after the hackathon ends.

## Joining Clubs
Forming connection with peers who have the same interests as you by going to clubs is significant in widening your network and immersing you in a practical setting that is hands-on.

Attend any club meetings from the Association for Computing Machinery[^7] by monitoring their Discord for any news of the first one. Another club to go to is the UW Data Science Society in the Seattle campus[^8]. The form to apply opens winter quarter, which will further connect you to more hackathons. 

# Assembling a Professional Profile
This section discusses activities that raises your overall credibility to potential employers in the field.

## Refining a Resume
A resume is an employer's first impression of your competency in the job they are hiring for. It is vital that a resume be tailored to every job you apply to so you can stand out.

Make a base resume with every one of your accolades and qualifications to edit for a specific job application. Schedule an appointment with career services and enroll in their Canvas course for a resume review[^9]. Get a second opinion from people with a technical basis by using the ACM Discord's resume review channel.

## Creating Personal Projects
Personal projects are an important part of your portfolio to show initiative and the application of technical concepts. Your first personal project should be simple and small in scale to get a product out there.

Start with an idea adjacent to something you have done before in a class (for example mapping out the weather with Folium). Make a schedule with milestones to keep you responsible to finish the project.
